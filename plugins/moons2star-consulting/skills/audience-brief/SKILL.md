# Audience Brief

*moons2star Consultant's Toolkit · created by Dr. Munmun Das · [moons2star.in](https://moons2star.in)*

**What this gives you:** a clear picture of who you must convince, what they currently believe, what will move them, and how to pre-wire the room, so the content is built for the reader rather than the author.

## The senior standard
1. **Decisions are social.** The formal decision-maker relies on advisers, so map the influence network, not just the org chart.
2. **Start from their beliefs.** Persuasion moves people from what they believe now. Write down both ends of the shift.
3. **Objections are information.** Anticipate them in the audience's words and prepare the evidence each needs. The best decks answer the first question before it's asked.
4. **Altitude and language fit the reader:** a CFO wants cash and risk; a CTO wants architecture and feasibility; a board wants strategy, risk and oversight.
5. **No surprises in the room.** Pre-wire key people, especially potential blockers, before the meeting.
6. **One ask.** A clear decision, phrased so "yes" is easy.

## Before you start: diagnose
- What decision do you need, and by when?
- Who attends, who influences from outside the room, and who can block?
- What happened the last time this topic or team came to this audience?
- Format and time: pre-read or live? 10 minutes or 60? In person or virtual?

## Method
1. **List the audience by role:** decision-maker; influencers (trusted advisers, committee chairs); blockers (budget owners, risk or compliance, affected units); gatekeepers (chief of staff, secretariat); implementers.
2. **Profile each:**
   - what they are measured on (KPIs, incentives);
   - their current priorities and worries;
   - their history with the topic;
   - their preferred style (data-first or story-first; detail or headline);
   - their likely first question.
3. **Beliefs shift:** "Now they think … → after, they need to think …". Keep it to one or two shifts per person.
4. **Objections and evidence:** the top 5 objections (in their words), with the evidence or concession that addresses each.
5. **Altitude and format:** the level of detail; numbers in the body vs the appendix; vocabulary to use or avoid; length; pre-read vs presentation; visual vs narrative.
6. **Politics:** alliances, rivalries, sensitivities (e.g. a proposal that reduces someone's remit); who should present which part.
7. **Pre-wiring plan:** who to meet before the meeting, in what order, with what message, and what you'll ask them.
8. **The ask:** one sentence, a decision with a date ("Approve X by Y"); plus fallback asks (e.g. approve a pilot).

## Judgement calls
- **Influencers can matter more than the decider.** A CFO's analyst or a board member's trusted adviser may shape the decision long before the meeting.
- **Concede early on small points** to build credibility for the big one.
- **Adjust messengers.** Sometimes a peer of the blocker, or a customer, should carry the argument.
- **Read the room's appetite for risk.** The same proposal framed as a "pilot" vs a "transformation" lands very differently.
- **Respect, don't manipulate.** Persuade with evidence and genuine benefits. Pre-wiring is about listening and adjusting, not lobbying for an ambush.

## Red flags
- An audience treated as one homogeneous group.
- Objections written in the presenter's language, not the audience's.
- No pre-wiring of people who can block.
- The ask buried, or "for information" when a decision is needed.
- Detail level set by what the team knows rather than what the audience needs.

## Variants by context
- **Board:** fiduciary lens; committee chairs as key influencers; papers circulated in advance; Chair and Company Secretary as gatekeepers.
- **Investors:** return, risk and growth; consistency with past guidance; credibility of management.
- **Regulators:** compliance, customer protection, systemic risk; a formal, evidence-heavy tone.
- **Internal ExCo:** resource trade-offs between peers; alignment with the CEO's agenda.
- **Clients:** business outcomes, risk of change, proof from similar clients, commercial terms.

## House rules
- Never invent facts, figures, quotes or sources. Label estimates "(est.)" and list assumptions openly.
- If key inputs are missing, ask up to 3 short questions, or proceed with clearly stated assumptions if the user wants speed.
- Answer first, then support. Plain English; define any jargon.
- Use the user's currency, units, terminology and context (e.g. ₹ and lakh/crore for Indian users).
- Do calculations with code or a spreadsheet when available, not mental arithmetic.
- For legal, regulatory, tax or accounting specifics, flag them for verification against current official sources and qualified advisers.
- Remind users not to paste confidential or personal data they are not permitted to share; suggest anonymising.
